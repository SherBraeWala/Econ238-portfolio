# KILL ONE OWL TO SAVE ANOTHER?

**ECON 238 · Week Seven · Drilling for Achievable Ideas**  
*A public, nonbinding ballot initiative about a feasible environmental tradeoff.*

> **The choice:** Should Oregon fund a limited, supervised program to remove barred owls in order to protect threatened northern spotted owls?

**A fact worth considering:** In a U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service field experiment, northern spotted owl populations in areas without barred owl removal declined by **about 12% per year**, while populations in treated areas stabilized. That finding comes from studied sites; it does not guarantee that this proposed pilot will produce the same result. [Source: U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service](https://www.fws.gov/question-answer/barred-owl-management-strategy-final-eis-faqs).

## THE PROPOSITION

### Oregon Spotted Owl Rescue Pilot

**The ballot question**

> Should Oregon appropriate up to $3 million from its General Fund for a three-year pilot (2027–2029) on consenting Oregon forestlands, using only federally approved specialists to remove up to 2,000 barred owls in federally designated management areas; reserve at least 20% of spending for independent spotted-owl monitoring; publish annual spending, removals, and survival results; and end the pilot December 31, 2029, unless renewed?

A **YES** vote supports this proposed, time-limited funding and management plan. A **NO** vote opposes it. **YES, IF…** means support only if specified conditions are met. This is an educational opinion ballot, **not an actual Oregon election or enacted policy**.

## WHAT'S THE DEAL?

Oregon would spend at most $3 million over three years from its General Fund, money unavailable for other public priorities, to contract a limited barred-owl removal and independent monitoring pilot. The maximum removal would be 2,000 barred owls — lives intentionally lost — in designated Oregon areas, subject to federal permits, consent from participating land managers, and strict specialist training. At least $600,000 of the proposed cap would support monitoring and transparent reporting. The intended benefit is better survival of threatened northern spotted owls, a native species threatened by barred-owl competition and habitat loss. Taxpayers pay; trained contractors earn fees; cooperating landowners accept oversight; barred owls bear the direct biological cost. These caps are proposed policy terms, not agency cost estimates. At full removal, $3 million implies $1,500 per bird including monitoring, not a quoted contractor cost. Existing habitat protections remain.

## THE BEST CASE FOR YES

Waiting has costs. Northern spotted owls compete with larger, more adaptable barred owls for food and nesting habitat. In the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's removal experiment, spotted-owl populations stabilized in removal areas but declined about 12% a year in matched untreated areas. This is direct evidence that targeted intervention can work in studied areas, not a guarantee for this pilot. The federal government already has an approved strategy and permitting framework; Oregon would fund a smaller, time-limited contribution rather than invent a technology. Requiring willing land managers, trained specialists, published results, and a firm spending cap makes the risk bounded and testable. A carefully evaluated pilot could buy time while forest habitat is protected and restored. The relevant comparison is not a painless solution: it is a realistic intervention versus ongoing decline.

## THE BEST CASE FOR NO

Barred owls did not choose to expand westward. Killing up to 2,000 intelligent wild birds is a serious animal-welfare cost, especially when human-caused habitat change also threatens spotted owls. The best experimental results may not scale to new sites, and removed barred owls may be replaced by newcomers, turning a temporary pilot into pressure for perpetual killing. Monitoring is expensive and difficult; population recovery can take far longer than three years. The state could spend the same $3 million on habitat conservation, fire resilience, or other species with less deliberate harm. Federal approvals also do not compel private owners to participate. A hard cap limits budget risk, but could produce a pilot too small to achieve meaningful landscape-scale benefits.

## WHERE IS THE BODY BURIED?

The weakest assumption is that a limited, three-year Oregon pilot will reproduce the gains seen in controlled removal experiments. Barred owls can recolonize, and the state might face an ugly choice: stop and lose progress, or repeatedly fund lethal intervention. The number of removals needed for durable improvement — and their cost per protected territory — is uncertain. The $3 million cap is not a validated cost estimate. Owls killed cannot benefit from later corrections. An ethically defensible pilot needs an explicit stop rule and publicly reported results, including disappointing findings.

## WHY ISN'T THIS ALREADY HAPPENING?

The federal strategy was adopted in 2024, but implementation is voluntary, locally negotiated, and constrained by available resources. That is the economic puzzle. Benefits from conserving native spotted owls are spread across future generations and the wider public; funding and administrative burdens fall on current budgets and specific land managers. The most visible cost — killing barred owls — is concentrated and emotionally powerful. Agencies face complex permits, contractor training, landowner consent, reporting duties, and political opposition. Meanwhile, habitat protection and barred-owl management compete for scarce conservation dollars even though both may be necessary. Evidence of ecological benefit does not itself create an appropriation, agreements, or political legitimacy. A three-year capped pilot tries to reduce commitment risk while exposing the moral tradeoff to public judgment.
