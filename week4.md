<p class="kicker">Econ 238, week four: extreme weather, adaptation and risk</p>
<h1>Surviving the storm, miscounting the heat</h1>
<p class="dek">Per person, tornadoes, floods and hurricanes kill about two-thirds fewer Americans than they did in the 1940s. The death tolls that alarm us most are measuring something else entirely.</p>
<p class="by">Arjun Aujla, Econ 238, University of Rochester, fall 2026</p>
<a class="dl" href="{{ '/assets/week4-poster.pdf' | relative_url }}">Download the 18 &times; 24 in poster (PDF)</a>
<p class='lede'>In July 2025, a flood in the Texas Hill Country killed at least 135 people. Disasters like that make it feel obvious that American weather is getting deadlier. I pulled 86 years of National Weather Service fatality counts to check that feeling against the record. The record says something different, and it also shows how easily the counting itself can mislead us.</p>
<h2>Divide by the people exposed</h2>
<p>From 1940 through 2025, tornadoes, floods and hurricanes killed an average of 240 Americans a year. The raw decade averages drift down
unevenly, from 262 a year in the 1940s and about 300 in the 1950s and 1970s to 195 in the 2010s. That is a real decline, but a modest one, and
single catastrophes swamp it. Katrina alone put 1,016 deaths in the NWS hurricane column in 2005, and the 2025 Texas flood helped push flood
deaths to 230, the most in a year since 1972.</p>
<p>Now divide by the number of people who could be hit. The U.S. population grew from about 140 million in the 1940s to 336 million in the
2020s. Deaths per million residents fell from 1.88 to 0.61 in the 2010s, a two-thirds drop, and the first six years of the 2020s, Texas flood
included, come in at 0.68. For the average American, the storm risk is about a third of what it was when their grandparents were young.</p>

<h2>Richer, better warned, and more exposed</h2>
<p>Economists split risk into the <em>hazard</em> (the storm), <em>exposure</em> (who and what is in its path) and <em>vulnerability</em> (how
badly the exposed get hurt). Exposure clearly rose: more people, more houses on coasts and floodplains, more wealth in harm's way. The IPCC's
2012 special report on extreme events (SREX) concluded that rising exposure of people and assets is the main cause of the long-term rise in
disaster losses. So the drop in deaths per person has to come from lower vulnerability: radar and warnings, building codes, cars and highways
to evacuate on, and air conditioning, which research links to most of the steep decline in U.S. heat deaths after 1960.</p>
<p>The economic reading is that rich societies buy safety, and they also buy exposure on purpose. People pay a premium to live near water and
in warm places. Dollar losses can rise while deaths fall, and that can be a rational trade rather than a failure. (Dollar damages are a topic
for a later week.)</p>
<h2>Moving toward the heat, then away</h2>
<p>If Americans moved toward danger and still died less, that is strong evidence of adaptation. For extreme heat, the best evidence I found is
Leduc and Wilson (Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco, 2024). They count each county's extreme-heat days (a daily average above 80&deg;F)
from NOAA data for 1951&ndash;2020 and compare them with Census net migration by decade, across every county in the contiguous U.S. Using net
migration rather than raw population growth keeps births and immigration from driving the result.</p>

<p>The answer depends on when and whom you look at. For fifty years Americans moved into heat as air conditioning spread across the South; by
the 2010s that pull was gone. The newest Census figures fit the fading: Florida's net domestic migration fell from 310,892 in 2022 to 22,517
in 2025, and the Midwest gained domestic migrants for the first time this decade. One year of state totals reflects housing and insurance costs
as much as heat, so I read it as consistent, not as proof.</p>
<p>For the storm story, this strengthens the adaptation reading: the big drop in deaths per person happened while Americans were moving into
hotter places. But it adds a warning. The people now leaving hot counties are disproportionately college-educated, so the people left behind
may be the ones least able to pay for protection.</p>
<h2>The heat line jumps. Check the ruler first.</h2>
<p>One or two degrees of warming sounds small because we picture the gap between two comfortable days. The mechanism is different. Bodies
survive heat by shedding it, and when it is hot and humid, sweating cools us less and heat strains the heart and kidneys. A warmer average
shifts the whole range of temperatures people experience, so the change shows up as more days past dangerous levels, and it hits hardest the
people who were already near their limit: the old, the sick, outdoor workers, people alone in overheated apartments.</p>
<p>The NWS record shows exactly that pattern. The 1995 spike (1,021 deaths, driven by Chicago's July heat wave) fell on people who were old,
isolated and without cooling. That points to cheap, targeted protection, like checking on people who live alone, keeping the power on and
getting people into cool rooms, rather than anything that requires changing the weather.</p>
<p>The 2020s look worse still: 382 heat deaths a year, against 115 in the 2010s. But the NWS's own tables show the count moving. The 2020
edition of its fatality table lists 51 heat deaths for 2020; the current edition lists 350 for the same year. The NWS summary for 2024 listed
177 heat deaths; the current long-run table lists 529. Some of the jump is weather. For 2020, most of it is recounting. Before reading a
trend, ask whether the ruler changed.</p>

<h2>Two rulers, two very different numbers</h2>
<p>Burke and fifteen coauthors (NBER Working Paper 34313, 2025) use decades of mortality records from 30 countries and estimate that
non-optimal temperatures cause about 5% of all U.S. deaths, roughly 120,000 a year, which would make temperature about the eighth most
common cause of death in the country. Across their countries the share runs from 5% to 12%. For scale: if the world has about 60 million
deaths a year, 5&ndash;12% would be 3 to 7.2 million, between one death in twenty and one in eight. That is my illustration, not a global
estimate from the paper. The NWS, meanwhile, counts about 620 U.S. deaths a year from every kind of weather combined.</p>
<p><strong>These numbers do not conflict, because they measure different things.</strong> The NWS counts deaths a forecaster can tie to a
specific event, such as a driver swept off a flooded crossing. Burke's number is <em>attributable</em> mortality: the gap between the deaths
that happened at the temperatures people actually experienced and the deaths estimated for a world where every day sat at the least deadly
temperature (about 20&ndash;25&deg;C). Most of those deaths are recorded under other causes, mainly heart and lung disease. From 1968 to 2019,
U.S. doctors listed temperature on only 68,200 death certificates, about 0.06% of all deaths and nearly 100 times fewer than the authors'
estimate. The measure is useful when explained. It is misleading if presented as millions of death certificates reading &ldquo;heatstroke&rdquo;
or &ldquo;hypothermia.&rdquo; The European heat-death estimates commonly cited (50,000 to 175,000 a year) are modeled the same
way, which is one more reason they cannot be set beside NWS counts.</p>
<p>Two findings upend the usual headlines. Cold kills at least five times as many people as heat in their data, and the deadliest days are
ordinary cold ones (5&ndash;10&deg;C), not the extremes that make the news.</p>

<h3>How to read the next weather-death headline</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Counted or modeled?</strong> A counted death is tied to a specific event. A modeled death is a statistical excess over what would otherwise be expected.</li>
<li><strong>Per person or total?</strong> A growing population pushes totals up even when individual risk falls.</li>
<li><strong>Did the counting change?</strong> Check which edition of the data you are reading.</li>
<li><strong>One event or a trend?</strong> A single flood or hurricane can dominate a whole decade.</li>
</ul>

<h2>What people value, and what protection costs</h2>
<p>We tend to treat heating as a necessity and air conditioning as a comfort. If cooling prevents deaths, that line needs rethinking, and so
does the idea that wealth alone buys safety. Burke's group finds that within the U.S. and Europe, richer places are <em>not</em> consistently
less vulnerable to temperature, and that Europe, cooler and wealthy, loses more than twice the U.S. share of deaths to temperature. They also
find little rigorous evidence that heat alerts, cooling centers or tree planting reduce deaths. The stronger evidence is for policies with
&ldquo;little explicit to do with climate&rdquo;: affordable health care, affordable energy and social insurance.</p>
<p>The intervention I find most interesting is health insurance. When Mexico and Colombia rolled out subsidized or universal coverage for
low-income households, the effect of moderate cold on mortality fell by at least half.</p>
<ul class="econ">
<li><strong>At what cost?</strong> More clinicians, treatment and administration, paid by taxpayers or premiums.</li>
<li><strong>Compared to what?</strong> Lives saved plus the health gains insurance brings all year, measured against other uses of the same money, such as heating assistance or home insulation. The UK's winter fuel payment, a cash transfer for heating, showed no measurable health effect.</li>
<li><strong>Who decides?</strong> Governments and insurers set coverage, but it only works if patients can actually get care when cold weather hits.</li>
<li><strong>How do we know?</strong> The coverage reached different places at different times, so researchers could compare similar areas with and without it. Whether the result carries over to another country's health system still needs its own evaluation.</li>
</ul>
<p>The appeal is that insurance targets the biggest part of the burden, older people on ordinary cold days, that heat alerts never reach.</p>
<h2>But wait</h2>
<p>Arguing with myself, the first inference I would challenge is the easy one: &ldquo;Americans are better protected from heat, so installing
air conditioning across Europe would solve Europe's problem.&rdquo; Even if AC explains part of the gap, the comparison does not tell us how
much. Housing, age, health care and energy prices all differ too, and owning an air conditioner is not the same as being able to afford to run
it. I would still support expanding cooling, but I would promise less and measure whether a given program actually reaches vulnerable people
and reduces deaths.</p>
<p>The second is the opposite trap: &ldquo;cold kills more than heat today, so warming must save lives.&rdquo; Today's total burden does not
tell us how much cold mortality falls and heat mortality rises with another degree, and that answer differs by place and population. Burke's
own data warn against complacency: the U.S. temperature burden was the same share of deaths in the 2010s as in the 1980s.</p>
<p>The third is my own headline. It rests on NWS counts, and the heat revision shows those counts move when someone looks harder. The
categories shift too: the NWS assigns hurricane deaths to wind only and files surge and rain deaths under flood. The combined storm total holds
up better, and a two-thirds drop per person is too large for bookkeeping alone.</p>
<p>So I narrow my claim. Storm deaths are a success story of wealth and warnings. Temperature is a large, mostly invisible and mostly
cold-weather killer, our headlines and policies aim at the wrong days, and neither air conditioning nor warming settles the question on its own.</p>
<p class="closer">The storms we photograph kill about 230 Americans a year. The ordinary cold days nobody photographs kill far more.
Good policy starts by counting both with the same care.</p>
