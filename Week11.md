# Fish for Plastic 🎣♻️
### Pay the world's fishermen to catch plastic instead of fish

**Econ 238 · Week 11 · Batsh!t Crazy Environmental Idea** · Arjun Aujla

---

## TL;DR

- **The problem:** Ocean plastic is *abandoned property*. Nobody owns it, and it's worth less than the cost of picking it up, so nobody does. At the same time, governments pay about **$22 billion a year** in harmful subsidies that push fishing fleets to empty the ocean of fish.
- **The idea:** Put a tiny **$50/ton levy on every ton of virgin plastic** made on Earth (about $23B a year), redirect the **$22B of harmful fishing subsidies**, and use the pool (about $45B a year) to **guarantee a buyback price for recovered plastic**. That turns the world's 3.3 million motorized fishing boats into the largest cleanup fleet ever assembled.
- **Why it works:** One policy hits **two tragedies of the commons at once**. Plastic comes out of the ocean, fishing pressure goes down, and the money flows to the poorest coastal communities, where most of the plastic is.

---

## 1. Why this makes me furious

Growing up in the Bay Area, I saw the beaches. Plastic in the ocean makes me angrier than almost any other environmental problem, because it's so *visible* and so *dumb*. We know what it is, we know where it comes from, and it just keeps floating there.

My first instinct for this assignment was honestly to **launch all our trash into the sun**. That idea dies fast once you run the numbers (see Section 3). But it asked the right question: *why is nobody taking the trash away?* The answer turned out to be the property-rights material we've been doing all semester. That's where Fish for Plastic came from.

---

## 2. The idea in one paragraph

Ocean plastic sits there because, economically, it's worthless: picking it up costs more than it's worth. Aluminum cans almost never end up floating in the ocean because they're worth money. So **change the price of plastic.** A global levy on virgin plastic production funds a **guaranteed price floor** for recovered plastic, paid in tiers depending on where it's recovered. The collection workforce already exists: the world's fishing fleet. That fleet is currently subsidized to *overfish*. Fish for Plastic **swaps the subsidy**. Same fishermen, same boats, same money, new target: *catch plastic, not fish*.

---

## 3. Why it sounds Batsh!t

- **It pays people to fish less.** Fishing subsidies are some of the most politically protected money on Earth.
- **It taxes plastic globally**, when the world has failed twice to even agree on a plastics treaty (December 2024 and August 2025, and talks were still deadlocked in 2026).
- **It treats trash as a commodity** and fishing fleets as sanitation workers.
- **Its scale is absurd:** thousands of different fleets, 190+ countries, and an ocean covering 70% of the planet.

**And the idea I threw out first (launching trash into the sun) shows what "actually crazy" looks like:**

| | Number |
|---|---|
| Earth's orbital speed you must cancel to fall into the sun | ~30 km/s (escaping the solar system is actually *easier*) |
| Global plastic production per year | ~460 million tons |
| Optimistic future launch cost (Starship-type) | ~$100/kg |
| Cost to launch one year of plastic | **~$46 trillion, about 40% of world GDP** |

Fish for Plastic costs about **$45B a year**, roughly **0.1% of the sun plan**, and it removes the plastic that matters (the plastic already in, or headed for, the ocean).

---

## 4. The diagnosis: ocean plastic is a property-rights failure

**Ocean plastic is res nullius, nobody's property.** Once a bottle is lost or a net is cut loose, ownership is effectively abandoned. The ocean outside national waters is itself an open-access commons. With no owner and no price, nobody has a reason to remove it.

**Demsetz's insight from class:** property rights (and effort to claim things) emerge when the value of claiming exceeds the cost. Right now, for ocean plastic, it doesn't:

| Material (Philippine junk shop prices) | Price per kg |
|---|---|
| Aluminum | ~$1.72 |
| PET plastic | ~$0.34 |

Aluminum is worth about **5× more** per kilo. That's why poor communities collect cans and leave the plastic. The trash problem is really a **price** problem.

**Why not just use Coase (sue the polluter)?** Because transaction costs are enormous. Millions of polluters, billions of victims, and no way to trace which bottle came from where. When Coasean bargaining fails, the textbook fallback is a **Pigouvian price**. Fish for Plastic is a global **deposit-refund system**: a tax on new plastic plus a subsidy for recovering it. Environmental economists (e.g., Fullerton & Wolverton) have shown this tax-plus-subsidy pair can do what a perfect Pigouvian tax on *dumping* would do, without having to catch anyone in the act of dumping.

**The second commons: fish.** Fisheries are the classic tragedy of the commons (Hardin). FAO's 2025 review found **35.5% of assessed fish stocks are overfished**, and governments make it worse: of an estimated **$35.4B** in global fishing subsidies, **$22.2B is capacity-enhancing** (fuel, boats, gear), mostly going to large industrial fleets. Taxpayers are literally paying for the race to empty the sea.

> **Two broken commons, one fix:** take money that currently makes the fish commons *worse* and use it to make the plastic commons *better*.

## 5. How it works

### Funding (about $45B a year)

| Source | Calculation | Annual $ |
|---|---|---|
| Virgin plastic levy | 460 Mt × $50/ton | **$23.0B** |
| Redirected harmful fishing subsidies | Sumaila et al. estimate | **$22.2B** |
| **Total fund** | | **$45.2B** |

**How big is the levy?** Virgin PET trades around **$900–1,250/ton** in 2026, so $50/ton is about a **5% increase** in resin cost. For a 20-gram water bottle, that's about **0.1 cents**. Consumers won't notice. Producers will notice that recycled plastic just got relatively cheaper.

### The buyback tiers (pay more where plastic is harder to get)

| Tier | Where | Bounty | Target volume | Annual cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 🌊 Coastal & river-mouth waters | Fishing boats working the 1,000+ rivers that carry 80% of ocean plastic | **$1.50/kg** | 3.3 Mt | $5.0B |
| 👻 Ghost gear & open ocean | Lost nets, traps, lines, gyre debris | **$5.00/kg** | 0.5 Mt | $2.5B |
| 🏞️ Land & river interception | Plastic collected before it hits water, in high-leakage regions | **$0.50/kg** | 20 Mt | $10.0B |
| 🔍 Verification, ports, audits | 10% of fund | — | — | $4.5B |
| 🐟 Fisher transition + waste infrastructure | Paid "fallow" fishing days, vessel buybacks, sorting & recycling plants in hotspot countries | — | — | $23.2B |
| **Total** | | | **~24 Mt/yr** | **$45.2B** |

### Verification (the part that makes or breaks it)

- At-sea tiers paid **only to registered vessels** with GPS/AIS tracks that show where they actually were.
- Plastic landed at **certified ports**, weighed, photographed, and logged on a public ledger (Plastic Bank already does this with an app and blockchain).
- **Random lab audits** for marine weathering (biofouling, UV degradation). Fresh, clean plastic gets the *land* price, not the *ocean* price.

### The crazy accelerator: Plastic Recovery Bonds

Waiting 20 years is too slow. So I'd **securitize the levy**: sell bonds backed by 20 years of levy revenue and spend the money *now*, the same way the US front-loaded production in WWII. At a 6% yield:

> $23B/yr × annuity factor 11.47 ≈ **$264 billion upfront**

That's 35× The Ocean Cleanup's entire $7.5B plan for the Great Pacific Garbage Patch, available in year one.

## 6. The numbers (back of the envelope)

### Can fishermen actually collect this much?

| Assumption | Value | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Motorized fishing vessels worldwide | 3.3 million | FAO SOFIA 2024 |
| Share that join | 10% → **330,000 boats** | assumption |
| Plastic collected per boat-day in hotspot waters | 50 kg | assumption; land-based pickers in Indonesia collect 50–100 kg/day |
| Cleanup days per year | 200 | assumption |
| **Per boat** | **10 tons/yr → $15,000/yr at $1.50/kg** | |
| **Fleet total** | **3.3 Mt/yr** | |

**Reality check:** the OECD estimates **1.7 Mt** of plastic flowed into the ocean in 2019 (other models put river emissions at **0.8–2.7 Mt**). So 3.3 Mt a year would **exceed annual inflow** and start drawing down the roughly **30 Mt stock** already in the sea.

**Honest counterpoint:** today's voluntary Fishing for Litter scheme in Scotland averages only about **276 kg per vessel per year** (2,650 t from ~480 boats over 20 years). That's plastic collected *incidentally* while fishing. My 10 t/yr assumes boats fishing *for plastic*, in the dirtiest waters, paid to do it. That's a 36× jump, and it's the biggest assumption in this project.

**$15,000 a year matters.** Most of the world's fishing fleet is small-scale and in Asia (about 71% of vessels), where a $15k bounty income would equal or exceed what many small fishers earn from fishing.

### Is it worth it?

| | Value |
|---|---|
| Damage per ton of ocean plastic (Beaumont et al. 2019) | $3,300–33,000 per ton per year |
| Damage avoided by removing 3.3 Mt/yr | **$11B–109B per year** |
| Cost of coastal tier | $5B |

Even at the *lowest* damage estimate, the coastal tier more than pays for itself. And **$22B of the funding isn't new money at all**: it's already being spent, mostly doing harm.

### Problems the program creates (with math)

| Side effect | Calculation | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Diesel emissions** from 330k boats | 330k × 200 days × 20 L × 2.68 kg CO₂/L ≈ **3.5 Mt CO₂/yr** | Small. Global plastics are about 3.4% of all GHG emissions, so this is a rounding error, and many of these boats would be burning fuel fishing anyway. |
| **Cobra effect** (people make plastic to claim bounties, the way Hanoi's 1902 rat-tail bounty led people to cut off tails and release the rats to breed) | Virgin plastic costs ~$1.00/kg; land bounty is $0.50/kg | Making new plastic to claim the bounty **loses $0.50/kg**. Gaming the *land* tier just means more plastic gets collected, which is the goal. |
| **Ocean-washing fraud** (claiming land plastic as ocean plastic for the $1.50 price) | Gap is $1.00/kg | Real risk → GPS-verified vessels + weathering audits. |
| **Plastic glut** in recycling markets | 24 Mt recovered vs ~29 Mt of recycled plastic production (OECD) | Recycled prices could crash. Most ocean plastic is low-grade anyway → energy recovery or secure landfill. |
| **Fish prices** rise short-term as effort drops | Less catch → higher price | Hurts poor coastal consumers. Offset with transition payments while stocks rebuild. |

## 7. Why it's not as crazy as it sounds

Every piece of this already works somewhere, just small:

- 🇩🇪 **Germany's bottle deposit** (€0.25) gets **98% of bottles returned**. Michigan's 10¢ deposit gets about 90%. **Price works.**
- 🇵🇭 **Plastic Bank** pays collectors in the Philippines, Indonesia, Egypt, Brazil and elsewhere a premium per kilo, tracked on an app. **Paying the poor for plastic works.**
- 🏴󠁧󠁢󠁳󠁣󠁴󠁿 **Fishing for Litter** has had fishing boats landing marine trash at Scottish ports since 2005, now about 480 vessels and 38 ports. **Fishermen will do it**, even for free.
- 🌐 The **WTO Agreement on Fisheries Subsidies** entered into force on **September 15, 2025**, the first global trade rule built around ocean sustainability. **Countries can agree to touch fishing subsidies.**
- 🌊 **The Ocean Cleanup** proved that removing plastic from the open ocean is technically possible.

Fish for Plastic just connects these: **deposit-refund pricing + Plastic Bank's payment rails + Fishing for Litter's fleet model + the WTO's subsidy reform.**

---

## 8. If it's so great, why hasn't anyone done it?

**How we handle the problem now:** single-use bag and straw bans (in 120+ countries), beach cleanups, NGO ocean sweeps, and a stalled UN treaty.

**Benefits of the current way:** bans are easy to pass and explain. Cleanups build awareness. Nobody's business model gets blown up.

**Shortcomings:**
- Bans target a sliver of plastic and the OECD says they aren't doing enough to reduce overall pollution.
- Only **9% of plastic is recycled**, and **22%** escapes waste systems entirely.
- Ocean cleanup run as charity is expensive: The Ocean Cleanup's $7.5B plan for roughly 79,000–100,000 tons in the Great Pacific Garbage Patch works out to about **$75–95 per kilo**. You can't donate your way out of a 30-million-ton problem.

**Incumbents, seen and unseen:**

| Loses | Wins |
|---|---|
| **Petrochemical producers** and oil-exporting states, which led the blocking at the treaty talks | **Small-scale fishers** in Asia and Africa (new income) |
| **Large industrial fleets** that get >80% of fishing subsidies today | **Recyclers & sorting plants** (guaranteed feedstock) |
| **Cleanup NGOs** whose model is donations (now competing with a market) | **Tourism & coastal economies** |
| Some **waste haulers** whose landfill business competes with recovery | **Junk shops & informal waste pickers** (unseen winners: roughly the people with the least today) |

The losers are concentrated, organized, and rich. The winners are spread out, poor, and far away. That's Public Choice 101, and it's exactly why it hasn't happened.

## 9. Chesterton's Fence: are we tearing down something we don't understand?

Two fences are being moved here, and both exist for real reasons:

1. **Fishing subsidies exist** to protect food security, rural jobs, and coastal communities that have few other options.
2. **Cheap plastic exists** because it preserves food, keeps medicine sterile, and is so light it *cuts* shipping emissions compared with glass.

**My answer: we're not tearing down the fences, we're moving them.** The *same* fishing communities keep getting the *same* money; they just earn it differently. And a 5% levy doesn't ban plastic for its useful purposes. It only charges it for the damage it does when it escapes. The real risk is in the *transition* (a bad rollout could hurt fishers before bounty income arrives), which is why I'd phase it in region by region over 5 years, starting with the worst plastic hotspots (the Philippines, Indonesia, and other Southeast Asian countries).

## 10. Incentives and unintended consequences

**How each actor's incentives change:**
- **Fishers:** a guaranteed cash crop that never runs out of season, and less pressure to overfish.
- **Plastic producers:** a reason to design recyclable, recoverable products. Recycled resin gets relatively cheaper.
- **Consumer brands:** pressure to switch to cans and glass where it's cheap, and to stop using hard-to-recover multi-layer packaging.
- **Governments in poor coastal countries:** revenue for waste infrastructure they couldn't afford.
- **Rich countries:** they produce most of the plastic and pay most of the levy, which is fair.

**Unintended consequences (near and far):**
1. **The plastic "fishery" depletes itself.** As collectors clear the easy hotspots, kilos per day will fall, just like a fish stock. The bounty will need to **rise over time**, or the program's success will show up as fewer people participating. That's a feature, not a bug: it means the ocean is getting cleaner.
2. **Marine bycatch.** Plastic-catching nets can trap the small floating organisms that live on the surface alongside the debris (a criticism already raised about open-ocean cleanup). This needs gear rules and avoidance of sensitive zones.
3. **Ghost-gear moral hazard.** If lost nets are worth $5/kg, would anyone "lose" nets on purpose? Fix: register and mark gear (FAO already has voluntary guidelines), and pay a fleet *nothing* for recovering its own registered gear.
4. **Fish stock rebound.** Less fishing effort lets overfished stocks recover, which eventually *raises* long-run catches.
5. **Political spillover.** If the fishing fleet becomes the cleanup fleet, the fishing lobby becomes an *ocean-health* lobby.

**External benefits:** less microplastic in seafood (and in us), safer beaches and tourism, fewer turtles and seabirds tangled in ghost nets, and income transfers to the poorest coastal communities, the people who live closest to the trash. **External costs:** a slightly higher price on plastic goods for everyone, and lost profits for the subsidized industrial fleets.

## 11. Biggest criticisms and how I'd answer them

**Criticism 1: "You'll never get 190 countries to agree."**
True: the UN treaty has failed twice. So skip the consensus treaty and use a **"climate club" approach**: a coalition (the EU, Japan, Canada, Pacific island states, coastal Asian and African countries) applies the levy and a **border adjustment** on plastic imports from non-members. The largest markets make it costly to stay out. The WTO fisheries deal proves countries can move on subsidies outside a full UN process.

**Criticism 2: "Verification will be a corrupt mess."**
This is the real risk. But the costs of monitoring have collapsed: satellite AIS tracking, cheap GPS, phone apps, and lab tests for ocean weathering already exist. Plastic Bank already runs a version of this ledger across six countries. I budgeted 10% of the fund ($4.5B) for verification alone.

**Criticism 3: "Just stop making so much plastic."**
I agree upstream reduction matters, and the levy does push that way. But even if production stopped tomorrow, about **30 Mt** is already in the ocean and **109 Mt** sits in rivers, still heading downstream. Somebody has to collect it.

## 12. The political map

Fish for Plastic **cuts across the divide**:
- **The right** likes that it's market-based, uses prices instead of bans, rewards work, and *cuts* wasteful subsidies.
- **The left** likes that it taxes polluters, cleans the ocean, and moves money to poor communities.
- **It angers:** petrochemical companies, oil-exporting states, and industrial fishing lobbies.
- **It delights:** small fishers, environmental groups, coastal tourism, recyclers, and anyone who has ever seen a turtle in a net.

## 13. What happened when I pitched it to people

I pitched Fish for Plastic to three of my roommates.

- **Grant (the skeptic):** His first question was whether ocean plastic is really spread out at all. Isn't a lot of it concentrated in specific places? If so, boats would have to travel huge distances to reach it, and that could sink the whole idea.
- **Flo (the believer):** He was in right away. He remembered vacation beaches where trash kept washing up and the sand was littered with plastic. His verdict: "save the turtles."
- **Josh (the realist):** He raised Grant's point too, but came down on the other side. Even if the plastic hot spots are remote and hard to reach, he said, cleaning them up is necessary because protecting ocean ecosystems matters too much.

**What I took from it:** Grant's objection is the strongest one I heard, and he's partly right. Plastic *does* concentrate. Gyres like the Great Pacific Garbage Patch sit thousands of miles from shore, and the most expensive plastic to collect is the plastic far out at sea (about $75–95/kg for The Ocean Cleanup). That's exactly why the bounty is **tiered**. The main fleet works **coastal and river-mouth waters**, where more than 1,000 rivers carry about 80% of the plastic entering the ocean and the boats are already nearby. Only the highest $5/kg tier goes after ghost nets and remote debris, and that tier pays more precisely *because* the trip is longer. The cheapest strategy is catching plastic before it ever reaches the gyre. Josh's point is what the higher tier is for: some of the cleanup is worth doing even when it's hard.

**Why I think the idea is unpopular:** The first reaction people have is "that's too far and too expensive," which is the same intuition that kept me thinking about the sun. People also tend to picture ocean plastic as one giant floating island, so cleanup sounds like an engineering moonshot instead of a pricing problem. And the people who'd lose (petrochemical producers and subsidized industrial fleets) are organized, while the people who'd win are scattered across poor coastal towns most of us never think about.

---

## 14. The risks I can't see

- **Knowable unknowns:** how fast hotspot collection rates fall; how much fraud survives audits; how fish prices respond.
- **Unknowable unknowns:** whether microplastics turn out to be much more harmful to human health than we think (which would make this *far* more valuable), or whether a cheap breakthrough in plastic-eating enzymes makes collection obsolete.
- **Biggest worry:** that a guaranteed market for collected plastic gives the plastics industry a moral license to *produce more* ("it'll get collected anyway"). Keeping the levy, and raising it over time, is the guardrail.

---

## 15. The bigger picture: where we need more innovation

The ocean is the biggest **unpriced commons** on Earth, and most of our environmental problems are the same problem underneath: **valuable things with no owner and no price**. We need more innovation in **commons governance**: ways to create prices and property rights where none exist. We have also barely tried **financial engineering for environmental assets**, like securitizing future cleanup revenue the way we securitize mortgages. I think people don't work on these problems because the payoff is spread across billions of people while the costs land on a few powerful incumbents. Nobody gets rich cleaning a commons, *unless someone builds the market first.* Fish for Plastic is that market.
