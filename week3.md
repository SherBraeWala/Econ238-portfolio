
# Can America Still Build?

**Permitting, lawsuits, and what it would take to build a new environmental economy.**

America wants a cleaner energy system: more transmission lines, nuclear plants, geothermal wells, and solar farms. But building almost anything large in the U.S. now takes years of environmental review and litigation. That is true even of projects that would *help* the environment. This page asks what would have to change for the country to actually build that new economy, and what we might lose if we make building easier.

## Why America Can't Build, and Why Fewer Lawyers Won't Fix It

Brian Potter, Doomberg, Dan Wang, and Philip Howard agree on the symptom: America has made building slow, costly, and unpredictable. They disagree on the disease. Potter locates it in the incentives of the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA). Because agencies can be sued for missing anything, they write "litigation-proof" documents that average 661 pages and take 4.5 years. Doomberg widens the lens to a "legal-industrial complex," noting that we graduate 35,000 lawyers a year and about 600 petroleum engineers. Wang calls the U.S. a "lawyerly society" next to China's "engineering state," and Howard argues that rules have replaced human judgment.

My view is that the problem is not the number of lawyers; it is the number of veto points. Every project touches many groups with concentrated stakes, and each has a tool to delay it. That makes infrastructure an O-Ring problem: fixing one link does little while others still fail. California's high-speed rail, approved by voters in 2008, shows it. Environmental review, land acquisition, and funding politics each stalled it independently.

The reform I would back is **standardizing environmental review**: a fixed, public checklist for each project category, so that an agency that completes it is legally presumed to have complied. This changes three sets of incentives. Agency staff stop padding documents to anticipate every possible lawsuit. Plaintiffs can no longer win by finding one unanalyzed study. Developers can finally price permitting risk before committing capital. The second-order consequence is the catch: the fight moves to the checklist itself, with every interest group lobbying to add its item, which is the veto-point problem again. The 2025 rescission of the Council on Environmental Quality's uniform NEPA rules moved in the opposite direction, toward dozens of agency-specific procedures, which I worry increases unpredictability rather than reducing it.

Originally I argued for simply limiting what people can sue over. My partner changed my mind. She pointed out that serious harms like cancer can surface years later, when affected families are fighting illness rather than filing lawsuits, and that whether a community can fight a project *beforehand* depends on its resources. Capping lawsuits would shift the cost onto exactly those communities. So my version keeps one protection intact: the right to sue over actual harm after the fact, even when the procedural checklist was followed.

Her other point shapes the design too. Asked whether she would trust an agency head to approve a pipeline near home, she said yes, if they had a track record. Then she added that she would not know if their record was bad, because nobody would tell her. Trust needs visible results. A standardized checklist, with outcomes published project by project, creates that record. Howard's model of empowered officials only works if citizens can check the officials' work.

The question that could change my conclusion: does NEPA analysis actually prevent harm? Potter cites a 1986 study finding that only 30% of environmental impact statements accurately predicted impacts, but also cases like Bolinas Lagoon, where review saved taxpayers $133 million. If strong evidence showed review routinely stopping serious harm, I would weigh preserving the full process more heavily.
