## An Envelope With No Letter

https://arjunaujla.substack.com/p/week-8-homework?r=9c1rw5&utm_campaign=post&utm_medium=web&showWelcomeOnShare=true

In October of 1990, an envelope arrived at the University of Maryland, addressed to an economist named Julian Simon. Inside was a check for five hundred seventy-six dollars and seven cents, and a sheet of metal prices. According to Simon, there was no letter. No congratulations, no concession, not even a "you got lucky."

The check came from Paul Ehrlich, a Stanford biologist and, at the time, probably the most famous environmental scientist in America. Ehrlich had written a bestseller predicting mass starvation. He had sat on Johnny Carson's couch and told millions of Americans that the world was running out of room and running out of stuff. And ten years earlier, he had put his money where his mouth was. He bet Simon that five common metals would get more expensive over the 1980s, because a growing planet would use them up.

He lost. Every one of the five got cheaper.

So here is the puzzle I want to work through. Why would the world's most famous prophet of scarcity agree to settle his argument with a bet on metal prices, of all things? And the stranger part: why, more than thirty years later, do Ehrlich and plenty of serious environmental scientists still believe he was basically right? A check for five hundred seventy-six dollars sounds like it should end an argument. It didn't. By the end of this lecture, I want to convince you that the check proves something real, and something much narrower than either side wanted it to prove.

A quick word about where this structure comes from. Arnold Kling, in his lecture "AIDS and the Blue Dress," starts with a juxtaposition that shouldn't make sense and then lets economics and culture explain it. He also has a character who does exactly the opposite of what his identity predicts: C. Everett Koop, the conservative, evangelical surgeon general who became the government's most forceful voice on AIDS education. This story has a Koop too. Hold on to that. We'll get there.

## The Celebrity of Scarcity

To understand the bet, you have to understand how big Paul Ehrlich was, and why.

In 1968, Ehrlich was a respected butterfly biologist at Stanford. The Sierra Club and Ballantine Books asked him to write a short, popular book on overpopulation. He and his wife Anne wrote it quickly, though the publisher put only Paul's name on the cover and picked the title: The Population Bomb. Its opening line announced that the battle to feed all of humanity was over. The book predicted that in the 1970s hundreds of millions of people would starve to death, no matter what crash programs were started.

The book sold in the millions. And then came television. Johnny Carson invited Ehrlich onto The Tonight Show in 1970, and he came back again and again, more than twenty times by most counts. Think about what that meant. The Tonight Show was not a science program. It was the most important stage in American popular culture, the place where comedians got discovered and where America went to bed. And here was a professor with a dry delivery, telling Carson's audience that the party was nearly over. Ehrlich's new organization, Zero Population Growth, was flooded with mail.

Why did this message land so well? Part of it was timing. The late 1960s were the moment when Americans started seeing the costs of growth with their own eyes: smog over Los Angeles, a river in Cleveland that caught fire, beaches covered in oil off Santa Barbara. The first Earth Day came in 1970. In 1972, a group called the Club of Rome published The Limits to Growth, which used early computer models to argue that the world was headed for collapse. In 1973, the movie Soylent Green imagined New York City in the year 2022 as a sweltering, starving city of forty million people. And then came the gas lines. The oil shocks of 1973 and 1979 made scarcity feel like something you could see at the end of your own street.

There's a status element too. Being worried about the planet was what thoughtful, educated people did. Having fewer children became a statement about what kind of person you were. Ehrlich himself had a vasectomy and talked about it publicly. If you believed in the population bomb, you were on the side of science and the future. If you didn't, you seemed, at best, naive.

And some of the predictions were extreme. Ehrlich endorsed a "triage" idea floated by other writers: that some countries, India among them, were beyond saving, and food aid should go only to countries that could still pull through. Around 1970, he told a British audience he would take even money that England would not exist in the year 2000.

Now, here's the economics hiding inside the doom. Ehrlich's argument was basically Thomas Malthus updated for the jet age. Malthus, back in 1798, argued that population grows faster than the food supply, so misery is the natural check on human numbers. Ehrlich added metals, energy, clean air and open land. More people means more demand for fixed stocks. Fixed stocks plus rising demand means rising prices and, eventually, shortages. That's a clean, intuitive story. It's also a story with a testable prediction in it. If the world really is running out of stuff, the stuff should get more expensive.

That was the opening a certain economist had been waiting for.

## The Man Who Changed His Mind

Julian Simon is the Koop of this story.

You might expect the man who bet against Paul Ehrlich to have always been a cheerful free-market type who never worried about population. He wasn't. In the 1960s, Simon was a business and marketing professor at the University of Illinois, and he was worried about population growth in poor countries. He did research on how to use marketing techniques to persuade families to have fewer children. In other words, Simon started his career working on the same project as the population-control movement.

Then he looked at the data. Simon went looking for evidence that population growth makes countries poorer, and he didn't find the clean relationship he expected. Countries with fast population growth were not reliably getting poorer than countries with slow growth. Over long stretches of history, as population rose, people mostly got richer, lived longer and ate better. And when he looked at the prices of raw materials over the long run, adjusted for inflation, they mostly went down, not up.

So Simon changed sides, which is about the hardest thing an academic can do. By 1980 he had become the loudest critic of the doom consensus. That year, he published an article in the journal Science with a title that basically picked a fight: "Resources, Population, Environment: An Oversupply of False Bad News." The response was furious. Letters poured in. Simon was treated as a crank, or worse, as someone who didn't care about suffering.

His argument came down to one idea, which he later put in the title of a book: the ultimate resource is people. When something gets scarce, its price goes up. A higher price gives people a reason to find more of it, use less of it, or invent something else. A copper shortage doesn't just sit there. It makes it profitable for someone to dig a deeper mine, recycle old wire, or figure out how to send phone calls through glass instead of copper. Each new person isn't only a mouth. They're also a brain and a pair of hands.

That sounds optimistic, but it's really a statement about incentives. Simon wasn't saying resources are infinite in some physical sense. He was saying that the useful services we get from resources depend on knowledge and prices, and that markets are very good at turning scarcity into a search for substitutes.

It's worth noticing how lopsided the two men's positions were. Ehrlich had a bestseller, a national organization and a recurring spot on late-night television. Simon had academic articles and an argument that many of his colleagues found offensive. Ehrlich's message fit the images people saw every night on the news: smog, oil slicks, crowded cities in poor countries. Simon's message was about something you can't put on camera, which is a price signal quietly changing the decisions of millions of people. One side had a picture. The other side had a graph. In a media culture built on pictures, that's a big handicap.

And Simon had one more trait that matters here. He was tired of losing arguments to people who were more famous than he was. So he did what an economist would do. He offered a bet.

## The Wager

Simon's offer was public, and it was generous. Pick any raw material you like, as long as the government doesn't control its price: copper, wheat, oil, anything. Pick any date more than a year out. If the inflation-adjusted price goes up, I pay you. If it goes down, you pay me.

Ehrlich accepted, saying, more or less, that he wanted in before other greedy people grabbed the offer. He brought in two colleagues to help choose: the physicist John Harte and a young energy and resources scholar named John Holdren. They chose five metals that seemed like safe bets on scarcity: copper, chromium, nickel, tin and tungsten. These were industrial workhorses, used in wiring, steel, cans and lightbulb filaments. Their supplies seemed finite, and their uses seemed to be growing.

The terms went like this. Ehrlich's side would hypothetically buy two hundred dollars' worth of each metal, a thousand dollars total, at September 1980 prices. On September 29, 1990, ten years later, they would check what those same quantities were worth, adjusted for inflation. If the basket was worth more than a thousand dollars, Simon would pay the difference. If less, Ehrlich would.

Notice what Simon gave away. He let the other side choose the materials. He let them pick the commodities they were most confident about. And he let them do it at a moment when doom felt obvious. 1980 was the tail end of the worst decade for raw materials in living memory. Inflation was running in double digits. Oil had spiked after the Iranian revolution. Metal prices were high. The Carter administration had just released the Global 2000 Report, which warned that if trends continued, the world in the year 2000 would be more crowded, more polluted and less stable in its resources. Simon was betting against the government, the popular culture and most of the scientific establishment at the same time.

And the world's population was growing fast. Over the decade of the bet, it would grow by more than eight hundred million people, roughly another India.

## What the Decade Did

So what happened to the metals?

The 1980s did almost everything Simon's theory said would happen, plus a few things that had nothing to do with his theory at all.

Start with the part that was luck, or at least macroeconomics. In 1979, the Federal Reserve chairman, Paul Volcker, set out to break inflation by driving interest rates extremely high. That gave the United States and much of the world a brutal recession in the early 1980s. When factories slow down, they buy less copper and nickel. When interest rates are high, holding stockpiles of metal gets expensive, so traders sell. And the inflation fears that had pushed commodity prices up in 1980 faded as Volcker won. Some of the high prices Ehrlich bought into were inflated by fear, and the fear unwound.

Then there was tin. For decades, tin prices were propped up by an international agreement run by the International Tin Council, which bought tin to hold the price up. In October 1985, the council ran out of money and collapsed. Tin prices crashed. That's not Malthus or Simon. That's what happens when a price-fixing arrangement can't pay its bills.

But underneath the luck, Simon's mechanism was working exactly as advertised. The high prices of the 1970s had done what high prices do. Mining companies opened new mines and improved old ones. Manufacturers learned to use less metal per product. Tin cans gave way to aluminum and plastic. Copper, the classic wiring metal, started facing competition from fiber-optic cable, which carries far more information through glass made from sand. Satellites carried calls that once needed copper lines. Engineers designed around the expensive stuff.

This is the key economic point. The high prices of the 1970s were not just a warning sign of scarcity. They were the cure for it. A high price is a reward offered to anyone who can find more or use less. And the world's miners and engineers went out and collected that reward.

By September 1990, all five metals were cheaper in inflation-adjusted terms than they had been in 1980. The thousand-dollar basket was worth about four hundred twenty-four dollars in 1980 money. Tin and tungsten had fallen the most, each by more than half. Ehrlich owed Simon the difference: five hundred seventy-six dollars and seven cents.

That's the envelope. And Simon, who was not modest about this, made sure everybody knew.

It's worth pausing on how the culture moved during those ten years, because it moved in Simon's direction, at least for a while. In 1980, the national mood was scarcity: gas lines, inflation, a president who had told Americans they needed to make do with less. By the middle of the decade, the mood had swung the other way. Ronald Reagan ran for reelection in 1984 on an ad campaign that announced it was morning again in America. That same year, Simon and the futurist Herman Kahn published a book called The Resourceful Earth, a direct rebuttal to the Global 2000 Report. Its argument was simple. If present trends continue, the world in 2000 will be less crowded in the ways that matter, less polluted, and richer in resources, not poorer.

The point here isn't that Reagan read Julian Simon, or that Simon caused the mood shift. It's that the same events that broke the metals prices, Volcker's victory over inflation, the fall in oil prices, the end of the energy crisis, also changed what Americans believed about the future. Scarcity stopped feeling like the natural state of things. And a message that sounded crankish in 1980 sounded almost obvious by 1990.

Notice the economic logic underneath that cultural shift. When oil prices fell in the mid-1980s, it wasn't because the earth had grown new oil. It was because high prices in the 1970s had pulled new supply out of the North Sea and Alaska, pushed carmakers to build smaller and more efficient cars, and convinced utilities to stop burning oil for electricity. The same mechanism that broke tungsten broke oil. In 1986, oil prices collapsed so badly that Texas, which had been booming a few years earlier, went into a deep regional recession. Scarcity had turned into glut, and the glut came straight out of the scarcity.

## The Bet Behind the Bet

If the story ended there, it would be a simple morality tale: the doomsayer lost, the optimist won, markets solve everything. That's how a lot of people told it in the 1990s, including a well-known New York Times Magazine story at the end of 1990 and a 1997 Wired profile that called Simon "The Doomslayer."

But here's the complication. Economists who later went back to the data found that if you run the same bet over other ten-year windows in the twentieth century, Ehrlich would have won a lot of them, by some estimates most of them. In the 2000s, for example, China's construction boom sent metal prices soaring. If the bet had run from 2000 to 2010, Ehrlich would very likely have been the one cashing the check.

So did Simon win because he was right, or because he was lucky about the decade? The honest answer is both. His long-run claim, that raw materials tend to get cheaper over very long periods as knowledge improves, holds up well. His specific ten-year bet leaned heavily on Volcker, a recession and a collapsing tin cartel. Ehrlich could fairly say he drew a bad decade. He could not fairly say the theory of rising scarcity had been confirmed.

The more interesting complication is about what was being measured. After losing, Ehrlich and his allies argued that metal prices were never the real issue. The real issues were the atmosphere, forests, fisheries, topsoil and species. In 1995, Ehrlich and the climate scientist Stephen Schneider offered Simon a new set of bets on fifteen environmental trends, including global temperatures, carbon dioxide in the atmosphere, fish catches and farmland per person. Simon turned them down. He said he would bet on direct measures of human welfare, like life expectancy and income, not on what he saw as indirect indicators.

Step back and look at what that disagreement is really about. It isn't optimism versus pessimism. It's about prices.

Copper has an owner. Tungsten has an owner. When they get scarce, somebody's price goes up, and somebody profits from finding more. That's why Simon's mechanism works for metals. Now think about the atmosphere. Who owns it? Nobody. When carbon dioxide piles up, no price goes up, and nobody gets paid to find more sky. Open-ocean fisheries, until someone writes rules, work the same way. Every boat has a reason to catch as much as it can, and the stock can crash without any price warning you in time. This is the core of environmental economics. Markets are excellent at managing scarcity in things that have prices and owners. By default, they are bad at managing things that don't.

So here's the irony of the bet. Ehrlich, the scientist who cared most about the unpriced parts of nature, chose to bet on the most thoroughly priced, owned and traded part of nature there is. He bet on the metals market. He walked onto Simon's home field and played by Simon's rules. If he had insisted from the start on betting about a fishery or the atmosphere, the result, and the 1990 headlines, could have looked very different.

And there's one more twist. John Holdren, the young scholar who helped pick the losing metals, did not end up as a footnote. He went on to become President Obama's chief science adviser. The side that lost the bet won the institutions. Losing five hundred seventy-six dollars cost the doom side almost no status at all. The culture had already decided who the serious people were, and a check in an envelope wasn't going to change that.

## What Nobody Could Have Known

It's easy to laugh at the predictions of 1968 now. But I want to be fair to the people living through that moment, because much of what proved them wrong wasn't visible yet.

In 1968, the Green Revolution was just getting started. The plant scientist Norman Borlaug had developed high-yield, disease-resistant wheat, and it was being planted in India and Pakistan at the very moment Ehrlich was writing. Borlaug won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1970. Within a decade, India's wheat harvests had grown enormously. The country Ehrlich had written off was on its way to feeding itself. Someone in 1968 could see the seeds, but not yet the harvests.

Nobody in 1968 could know how fast birth rates would fall, either. Demographers call it the demographic transition. As countries get richer, as fewer children die young, and as women get educated and go to work, families choose to have fewer kids. In 1968, global fertility was around five children per woman. Today it's roughly half that, and many rich countries now worry about too few children, not too many. Population did keep growing. It has more than doubled since the book came out. But the explosion slowed on its own, mostly because of choices families made, not because of famine.

The mistaken expectations weren't only on the doom side. Simon and his allies didn't fully see that the big environmental problem of the next century would be about sinks, not sources. In 1980, the worry was running out of things to dig up. Today, the worry is running out of places to put what we burn. Climate change is exactly the kind of unpriced problem Simon's bet couldn't see.

And there was a darker mistaken expectation in the population-control era. Fear of the population bomb helped justify some ugly policies. During India's Emergency in the mid-1970s, the government ran sterilization campaigns that sterilized millions of people, many of them poor men pressured or coerced. China began its one-child policy in 1980, the same year Simon and Ehrlich signed their bet. Ehrlich didn't run these programs. But they grew out of a moment when many people sincerely believed human beings themselves were the problem. That belief had real costs, and they were paid by people who had very little say.

## Back to the Envelope

So let's come back to the envelope: a check for five hundred seventy-six dollars and seven cents, a list of metal prices, and no letter.

At the start, I asked two questions. Why would the most famous prophet of scarcity settle his argument with metal prices? And why do so many people think he was basically right even though he lost?

Here's my answer to the first. Ehrlich bet on metals because in 1980 nearly everyone, him included, pictured scarcity as running out of things you dig out of the ground. Gas lines, the Club of Rome, the Global 2000 Report: the culture of the 1970s had taught Americans to imagine scarcity as an empty pump or an empty mine. Ehrlich bet on the version of his fear that the culture had made vivid. And that happened to be exactly the version markets are best at fixing.

My answer to the second is that the check measured something real, but narrow. It measured whether a market with prices and owners would respond to scarcity by finding more and using less. The answer was yes, emphatically, and that was a lesson the doom movement badly needed. Population growth did not starve the world, and the prophets who said it would were wrong, sometimes in ways that hurt real people.

But the check didn't measure the atmosphere, or the ocean, or species that have no market price. Those are the places where Ehrlich's instincts were closer to right, and where Simon's mechanism doesn't kick in on its own, because nobody's price goes up.

So here's my verdict. On the question that was actually bet, Simon was right, and not only because of luck. Over the long run, human ingenuity, driven by prices, has beaten resource scarcity again and again. Ehrlich's mistake wasn't caring about the planet. It was ignoring incentives, and assuming people would sit still while things ran out. But the most useful lesson of the bet is one neither man fully meant to teach. Scarcity is a problem we're good at solving when it has a price, and bad at solving when it doesn't. The real policy question isn't whether to trust markets or fear growth. It's how to put prices and property rights on the things that don't have them yet.

You can see this lesson at work in the policies that actually succeeded after the bet. When the United States wanted to cut the sulfur dioxide that caused acid rain, it didn't just order power plants to stop. In 1990, the same year the check arrived, Congress created a market for pollution permits, so that cutting emissions became something a power plant could profit from. Emissions fell faster and more cheaply than most people expected. That's Simon's mechanism, applied to Ehrlich's kind of problem. Somebody had to create the price first. Once the price existed, ingenuity went to work on it, the same way it had gone to work on tin and tungsten.

That's a less dramatic ending than "the doomsayer lost." But it's the one that has held up.

And the envelope with no letter? I think it says something too. Ehrlich never conceded the larger argument, and he never had to. In the culture, he had already won it: the Tonight Show couch, the bestseller, the colleague who would one day advise a president. Simon won the bet and spent the rest of his life being treated as the contrarian. The check proved that being right about prices and being right about what people want to believe are two very different things.

Paul Ehrlich lost five hundred seventy-six dollars. He kept the argument.
